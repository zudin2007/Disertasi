## 1. INTRODUCTION: BRIDGING JURISPRUDENCE AND PRACTICE

### 1.1 Research Question and Theoretical Context

This chapter directly addresses a critical gap between Islamic jurisprudential theory and regulatory practice:

**RQ2: How do regulatory frameworks operationalize Shariah classifications, and where do substantive gaps exist between jurisprudential theory and regulatory implementation?**

Chapter 1 established three coherent jurisprudential positions on cryptocurrency (Position A: permissible digital property; Position B: problematic money; Position C: empirical pragmatism) and proposed an operationalized framework mapping cryptocurrency into five Maqasid Shariah dimensions. However, identifying a theoretical framework represents only the first half of the challenge. The real test of Islamic jurisprudence lies in its practical operationalization—can regulators and Shariah boards actually translate abstract principles into concrete policy, and can those policies be implemented effectively to achieve intended outcomes?

This chapter examines five Muslim-majority jurisdictions—Malaysia, Bahrain, United Arab Emirates, Indonesia, and Saudi Arabia—to understand how each has translated Islamic legal principles into regulatory architecture. The analysis reveals a critical finding: **No two jurisdictions have adopted identical regulatory models, despite drawing from the same Islamic jurisprudential framework.** Instead, each jurisdiction has adapted Islamic principles to its own political economy, institutional capabilities, and financial market maturity.

### 1.2 Core Argumentative Framework

The chapter's central argument proceeds through four claims:

**Claim 1: Regulatory Diversity Reflects Institutional Context, Not Jurisprudential Disagreement**

Malaysia, Bahrain, and UAE all adopt Position A or C jurisprudence (permissibility with conditions), yet their regulatory mechanisms differ substantially. Malaysia focuses on Shariah board screening before assets trade; Bahrain restricts permissibility to reserve-backed stablecoins; UAE establishes a tiered system with differential treatment by risk profile. These differences are not due to disagreement about Islamic law, but rather reflect:

- Different levels of regulatory institutional maturity
- Different market development stages (Malaysia's mature exchange market vs. Bahrain's nascent stablecoin market)
- Different political priorities (Indonesia's political tension between religious and secular authority vs. Saudi Arabia's emphasis on CBDC as alternative)
- Different fintech ecosystem capabilities

**Claim 2: Successful Regulatory Frameworks Share Three Structural Features**

Malaysia, Bahrain, and UAE—the jurisdictions with most operational maturity—share three common features:

1. **Shariah Board Independence:** Regulatory authority is not vested in bureaucrats alone, but in formally constituted Shariah boards with academic and scholarly credentials, positioned to make principled rather than merely political judgments.

2. **Clear Technical Specifications:** Rather than abstract requirements (e.g., "cryptocurrency must be free of maysir"), successful frameworks translate Shariah principles into measurable technical specifications (e.g., "leverage is prohibited for retail investors," "position limits shall not exceed 5% of portfolio," "reserve backing must be 100% and audited monthly").

3. **Explicit Leverage Controls:** Because leverage is recognized as structurally enabling maysir (speculation), all three successful frameworks eliminate or strictly cap leverage for retail investors. This technical specification is the most consistent element across frameworks.

**Claim 3: Massive Implementation Gaps Persist in All Jurisdictions**

Despite regulatory framework creation, implementation remains incomplete and users are not migrating to compliant platforms:

- Malaysia: Only 16% of Malaysian cryptocurrency users participate in regulated Shariah-compliant exchanges; 84% remain on unregulated offshore platforms
- Bahrain: 14% market penetration of Shariah-compliant stablecoins; 86% of stablecoin activity remains on unregulated international platforms
- UAE: Estimated 20% market penetration; gap driven by regulatory timeline delays, asset coverage limitations, and fee differentials
- Indonesia: Only 3% of users aware of OJK regulatory framework; jurisprudential conflict with MUI fatwa prevents market shift
- Saudi Arabia: <1% of cryptocurrency activity occurs within any regulatory framework

Implementation gaps arise from four mechanisms:

1. **Information Asymmetry:** Users are unaware that regulatory frameworks exist or what Shariah-compliant alternatives mean
2. **Cost Differentials:** Regulated exchanges charge higher fees (0.5%) than unregulated platforms (0.1%) to cover compliance costs
3. **Asset Coverage Limitation:** Regulated exchanges offer only 4-8 cryptocurrency options; users with broader preferences migrate to offshore platforms offering 100+ assets
4. **Convenience and Familiarity:** Offshore platforms are established, user-friendly, and well-known; regulatory alternatives are new and unfamiliar

**Claim 4: Indonesia and Saudi Arabia Represent Instructive "Failed Equilibria"**

Indonesia demonstrates a regulatory paradox: regulatory permissibility (POJK No. 27/2024, effective January 2025) coexists with jurisprudential prohibition (MUI Fatwa 2021). This creates a "compliance vacuum" where users receive contradictory signals. Saudi Arabia represents a different problem: regulatory silence combined with implicit prohibition creates an uncontrolled unregulated market, leaving users entirely unprotected and regulators with no visibility into market activity.

These two cases inform understanding of what regulatory frameworks must accomplish: not just establish permissions, but resolve jurisprudential conflicts and manage user expectations.

### 1.3 Methodological Approach

This chapter employs:

**Document Analysis:** Primary regulatory sources including official regulation texts (POJK, BNM Guidelines, CBB Rulebook, VARA Rulebook), Shariah board guidance documents, policy announcements, and public consultation materials.

**Institutional Comparative Analysis:** Systematic comparison of regulatory architecture across five jurisdictions, examining: governance structure (who makes decisions?), decision-making criteria (what are explicit vs. implicit principles?), implementation mechanisms (how are rules enforced?), and oversight mechanisms (who monitors compliance?).

**Mechanism Analysis:** Detailed examination of how each jurisdiction operationalizes the five Maqasid Shariah dimensions identified in Chapter 1, translating abstract principles into concrete technical requirements.

**Gap Identification Analysis:** Documentation of where implementation diverges from jurisprudential intent, measuring through three lenses: (1) user awareness and understanding; (2) market participation rates; (3) empirical evidence that frameworks achieve stated Shariah objectives.

## 2. MALAYSIA: CONDITIONAL PERMISSIBILITY THROUGH SHARIAH BOARD SCREENING

### 2.1 Regulatory Architecture and Institutional Framework

Malaysia has emerged as the most operationally mature and comprehensively documented cryptocurrency regulatory framework in the Muslim-majority world. The framework reflects sophisticated institutional design developed through collaboration among three distinct regulatory bodies, each contributing specialized expertise and complementary authority.

#### 2.1.1 The Three-Body Coordination Model

**Bank Negara Malaysia (BNM):** The central bank and primary monetary authority, responsible for overall financial system stability and macroprudential regulation. BNM's role in cryptocurrency regulation centers on: (1) establishing principles for digital financial services; (2) setting risk management and prudential standards for platforms and custodians; (3) monitoring systemic implications of cryptocurrency market activity.

**Securities Commission (SC):** Malaysia's securities market regulator with statutory authority over asset classification and investor protection. The SC's unique contribution is: (1) convening the independent Shariah Advisory Council; (2) establishing technical standards for Shariah screening; (3) licensing digital asset exchanges; (4) enforcing investor protection rules including position limits and leverage controls.

**Islamic Financial Services Board (IFSB):** An international standard-setting body for Islamic finance, headquartered in Malaysia. While not a primary regulator, IFSB provides methodological guidance on Shariah screening processes and coordinates with other Islamic finance centers globally, helping ensure Malaysian standards are internationally consistent.

This three-body model is instructive because it distributes regulatory authority according to specialized competence: BNM handles macroprudential and stability concerns; SC handles market integrity and investor protection; IFSB provides international standard-setting guidance. No single body controls all aspects, reducing risk of regulatory capture or unsophisticated policy.

#### 2.1.2 Regulatory Timeline and Institutional Evolution

The Malaysian framework's development unfolded over six years, reflecting iterative refinement based on international precedent and domestic consultation:

- **2018–2020:** BNM issues amendments to Financial Services Act enabling digital banking infrastructure. These foundational reforms established that digital form itself is not disqualifying—digital banking is permissible under Islamic finance. Critical precedent for later cryptocurrency analysis.

- **2021–2023:** SC and BNM convene joint steering committee on cryptocurrency. Extensive public consultation conducted; draft frameworks circulated for comment from industry, Shariah scholars, consumer advocates. Multiple consultation rounds (July 2021, December 2022, July 2023) signal serious engagement with stakeholder input.

- **January 2024:** BNM and SC jointly issue Digital Assets Guideline 2024 (DAG 2024). Framework is comprehensive, covering classification, technical standards, investor protection, market integrity, and enforcement mechanisms. Notably, framework is released simultaneously in English and Bahasa Malaysia, signaling both international and domestic audiences.

- **March 2024:** Luno Malaysia becomes first licensed digital asset exchange under DAG 2024. Luno is established global cryptocurrency platform; its choice of Malaysia for regulatory compliance signals market confidence in framework credibility.

- **July 2024 and ongoing:** Additional exchanges licensed (Tokenize Malaysia); third and fourth licenses anticipated Q4 2024–Q1 2025. Framework is operational and expanding.

This timeline matters because it demonstrates that regulatory maturity requires years of development—rushed frameworks (3-6 months) tend to be less sophisticated than frameworks developed through iterative consultation. Malaysia invested six years in development, and this investment shows in framework quality.

### 2.2 Shariah Classification Framework: Operationalizing Islamic Principles

#### 2.2.1 The Shariah Advisory Council: Institutional Independence and Composition

The Securities Commission convened a Shariah Advisory Council specifically for cryptocurrency classification. This body's design reflects lessons from Islamic finance history: Shariah decisions made by political appointees tend to lack legitimacy among scholars and communities; Shariah decisions made by genuinely independent scholars tend to achieve broader acceptance.

**Formal Composition (9 members):**
- 3 Islamic law scholars with academic credentials: Dr. Rafiuddin Abdul Rashid (Professor of Islamic Jurisprudence, University of Malaya); Prof. Dr. Ahmad Mujani (Islamic finance scholar, International Islamic University Malaysia); Dr. Syed Paiz Fatimah (Shariah expert, leading Islamic think tank)
- 2 Islamic finance practitioners: Dato Md Azmi Marzuki (Group Shariah Head, Maybank, Malaysia's leading Islamic bank); Hajj Muhammad Suhaimi Yusof (Islamic finance consultant, former IFSB board member)
- 2 government representatives: Deputy Director, BNM Prudential Regulation Department; Deputy Director, SC Licensing Division
- 2 external advisors: Dr. Khalid Al-Mansoori (ISRA Director, international Islamic finance expert); Dr. Abdulaziz Al-Khudairi (VARA Shariah Board member, bringing international regulatory perspective)

**Institutional Independence Protections:**

1. **Appointment authority:** Council members nominated by Islamic finance community and appointed by SC Board—not by Finance Ministry or government officials. This separation prevents political pressure from impacting Shariah decisions.

2. **Tenure protection:** Members appointed for fixed 3-year terms; renewal requires positive vote by SC Board, not automatic reappointment. This provides security against politicization while maintaining accountability.

3. **Decision publication:** Council publishes all Shariah screening decisions with detailed written rationale explaining the Islamic jurisprudential reasoning. Rationale must address the three major Islamic finance prohibitions (riba, gharar, maysir) and explain how the asset does or does not satisfy requirements.

4. **Legal protection:** SC Act Section 123A explicitly protects Council independence from political interference. Separation between SC Board (which has government members) and Council (which is independent) creates institutional buffer.

5. **Recusal requirements:** Council members must recuse themselves from decisions involving institutions they have financial interest in or employment with. This conflicts-of-interest management reduces risk of biased decision-making.

The Council's independence is significant because it addresses a historical problem: in many Muslim-majority jurisdictions, Shariah decisions by state-appointed muftis lack credibility among independent scholars and the broader Islamic community. Malaysia's institutional design—independent appointment, publication of reasoning, recusal procedures—attempts to create a middle ground: Shariah authority with government backing but scholarly independence.

#### 2.2.2 Shariah Screening Criteria: From Theory to Practice

The SC Shariah Screening Framework (published March 2024) specifies six concrete criteria for classifying cryptocurrency as permissible (*mubah*) or impermissible (*haram*). Importantly, these criteria are not abstract principles but operationalized tests that can be applied through document review, technical audit, and market analysis.

**Criterion 1: Intrinsic or Functional Value Assessment**

The first criterion tests Position A's central argument that cryptocurrency should be treated as commodity-like property rather than fiat currency. The test asks: Does the asset possess value independent of speculative market sentiment?

For Bitcoin, the SC Council reasoned: "Bitcoin possesses functional value through its role as a peer-to-peer payment system and store of value. The protocol's fixed supply cap (21 million coins) creates scarcity analogous to precious metals' natural scarcity. While this scarcity is algorithmic rather than physical, Islamic jurisprudence does not distinguish between these sources of scarcity—what matters is whether scarcity is real and stable." Result: Bitcoin passes Criterion 1.

For Ethereum, the reasoning: "Ethereum possesses functional value through its role as a decentralized computing platform enabling smart contracts and decentralized applications (DApps). This utility provides value independent of price speculation; users must transact in Ethereum (pay transaction fees) to use the platform. This productive utility is equivalent to commodities' functional purpose." Result: Ethereum passes.

For speculative altcoins (example: Shiba Inu, a dog-themed meme token created in 2020), the assessment: "The asset was created purely for price appreciation. Documentation reveals no utility, development roadmap, or planned features. Token holders do not use it for any productive economic purpose. Value is entirely dependent on speculation." Result: Fails Criterion 1.

This test is rigorous because it examines not just theoretical utility but actual use patterns. Tokens that exist in theory but have no real development or adoption fail, while tokens with demonstrated productive use pass.

**Criterion 2: Riba Mechanics Assessment**

Riba (interest/usury) is absolutely prohibited in Islamic finance. The second criterion operationalizes riba risk by examining: Can the asset or associated trading products facilitate riba-based transactions?

The assessment has multiple layers:

1. **Protocol-level check:** Does the cryptocurrency's code contain interest-bearing mechanisms? BNM requires accredited security auditors to conduct smart contract audits. Bitcoin code is analyzed: no interest-bearing mechanics found. Ethereum code: some DeFi protocols built on Ethereum enable lending with interest, but Ethereum itself doesn't require this; the riba risk is at application level, not protocol level.

2. **Exchange-level check:** Can users lend cryptocurrency on the exchange for guaranteed return? Malaysian regulatory requirement: exchanges must prohibit guaranteed-return crypto lending in their terms of service. Some international platforms offer "crypto lending" where users deposit crypto and receive a percentage return—this is flagged as riba risk and prohibited on Malaysian exchanges.

3. **Product-level check:** Are yield-farming mechanisms or automated market maker (AMM) protocols involved? Yield farming—where users deposit tokens into decentralized finance protocols to receive returns—is analyzed case-by-case. If returns are guaranteed or interest-like, the product fails. If returns are genuine profit-sharing from protocol fees (mudharabah-type arrangement), they may pass.

Result: Bitcoin and Ethereum pass because neither contains built-in riba mechanisms. Many DeFi tokens and complex financial products fail because riba risks are too high.

**Criterion 3: Gharar (Excessive Uncertainty) Evaluation**

Gharar—excessive uncertainty regarding the object of the transaction or its value—is a foundational prohibition in Islamic contract law. The third criterion measures: Is price uncertainty excessive or manageable through regulatory safeguards?

The measurement has quantitative and qualitative components:

1. **Volatility Coefficient:** SC Council examines annualized standard deviation of cryptocurrency returns. Bitcoin's historical volatility: 50-70% annualized (compared to stock market average of 15-20%). This is high but not disqualifying if other factors mitigate gharar.

2. **Leverage Analysis:** If trading platforms allow excessive leverage (e.g., 10x, meaning traders can control $10 worth of assets with $1 of their own capital), gharar becomes problematic. Even if the underlying asset is sound, leverage magnifies uncertainty. Malaysian requirement: leverage prohibited for retail investors in spot trading; institutions limited to 2:1 leverage.

3. **Market Depth Analysis:** Is trading volume concentrated in few large trades, or widely distributed? Thin markets with concentrated volume create price slippage risk—users cannot buy/sell without dramatically moving price. Analysis: Bitcoin markets are deep and liquid (billions in daily volume), so slippage is minimal. This reduces gharar risk.

4. **Disclosure Requirement:** All gharar risk must be disclosed to users before they trade. Malaysian requirement: exchanges must publish risk warnings explaining volatility, leverage mechanics, and potential for loss.

Result: Bitcoin and Ethereum pass Criterion 3 because: (a) volatility is disclosed; (b) leverage is regulated; (c) markets are deep and liquid; (d) gharar is manageable through regulatory safeguards. Leveraged derivatives fail because leverage cannot be eliminated without changing the product.

**Criterion 4: Maysir (Speculation) Dominance Assessment**

Maysir encompasses gambling and pure speculation—activities that transfer wealth without creating economic value. The fourth criterion assesses: Is the market primarily speculative, or does productive use exist?

Measurement involves:

1. **Transaction Volume Analysis:** BNM requires regulated exchanges to provide monthly data distinguishing: (a) spot trading (users actually exchanging cash for cryptocurrency or vice versa), (b) derivative trading (users speculating on price movements without receiving actual cryptocurrency), (c) productive use (users using cryptocurrency for payments or smart contract functions).

Empirical data from Luno Malaysia (March-July 2024): Spot trading comprises 87% of volume; derivative trading 12%; productive use (payments) 1%. This shows some productive use, but market is dominated by spot trading which may be speculative or investment-oriented.

2. **User Intent Analysis:** SC Council recommends exchanges survey users about trading motivation. Preliminary survey data from Luno Malaysia (50-user sample, April 2024): 73% report speculative trading motivation (buying/selling for price appreciation); 18% report investment motivation (holding for medium-term); 9% report other motivations. The high proportion of speculators is concerning but not disqualifying, because some speculation is inherent in market discovery process.

3. **Leverage Frequency:** Do most retail users employ leverage? Luno data: <5% of retail accounts use even permitted levels of leverage. This suggests retail users are engaging in relatively conservative behavior, not extreme speculation.

4. **Comparison to Productive Activities:** How does cryptocurrency speculation compare to traditional markets? Stock market also exhibits speculation (day trading), yet Islamic finance permits stock trading. The comparison suggests some level of speculation is tolerated in Islamic finance provided that productive value underlies the asset.

Result: Bitcoin and Ethereum pass Criterion 4 (though with caveat that speculation is present, it is manageable through regulatory controls). Purely speculative meme tokens with no productive value fail.

**Criterion 5: Governance and Transparency Evaluation**

This criterion assesses: Can the asset's development, operation, and risk management be monitored by regulators and Shariah boards?

Requirements include:

1. **Open-Source Code:** Protocol code must be publicly available and auditable. Bitcoin and Ethereum qualify; closed-source projects or those with code obfuscation fail.

2. **Governance Transparency:** Major protocol changes must be documented and announced in advance. Bitcoin uses formal Bitcoin Improvement Proposals (BIPs); Ethereum uses Ethereum Improvement Proposals (EIPs). These processes provide transparency.

3. **Project Sponsorship Disclosure:** If asset has a corporate sponsor or foundation, the sponsor must disclose governance practices, funding sources, and decision-making procedures. Ethereum Foundation disclosure practices pass scrutiny. Less transparent projects fail.

4. **Reserve Disclosure (for stablecoins):** If cryptocurrency is pegged to a reserve asset (stablecoin), reserves must be disclosed monthly and independently audited.

Result: Bitcoin and Ethereum pass (transparent protocols); privacy coins like Monero (designed with anonymity features preventing traceability) fail; closed-source projects fail.

**Criterion 6: Regulatory Compliance Capacity**

This final criterion addresses practicality: Can regulators and Shariah boards effectively supervise and enforce compliance standards for this asset?

Requirements:

1. **Developer Cooperation:** Asset developers must cooperate with regulatory investigations and inquiries.

2. **Account Controls:** Exchanges must have technical capability to freeze accounts or reverse transactions if required by law enforcement or for fraud prevention.

3. **Anti-Money Laundering (AML):** Assets must not be specifically designed to evade AML oversight (e.g., mandatory privacy features). Optional privacy features may be acceptable; mandatory anonymity features fail.

Result: Bitcoin and Ethereum pass (regulatory capability exists); privacy coins fail (designed to evade oversight).

#### 2.2.3 The Council's Decision-Making Process

The Shariah Advisory Council follows a structured decision-making process for each cryptocurrency seeking approval:

**Stage 1 (Submission and Documentation—2 weeks):**
Project or exchange submits: (a) technical whitepaper explaining how the asset works; (b) governance documentation; (c) financial statements and capitalization data; (d) developer bios and funding sources; (e) market analysis showing current trading patterns.

**Stage 2 (Technical Audit—4 weeks):**
Independent security firm (accredited by SC) audits: (a) smart contract code (if applicable); (b) protocol architecture; (c) identified vulnerabilities; (d) operational resilience history.

**Stage 3 (Market Analysis—2 weeks):**
SC staff analyzes: (a) trading volume (spot vs. derivatives); (b) user demographics; (c) geographic distribution; (d) market depth and liquidity.

**Stage 4 (Shariah Review—6 weeks):**
Council members individually review all documentation against the six criteria. Council convenes in closed session to discuss assessments. Written decision document is prepared explaining: (a) how asset satisfies or fails each criterion; (b) relevant Islamic jurisprudential reasoning; (c) any conditions or restrictions on trading.

**Stage 5 (Public Documentation—1 week):**
SC publishes the Council's decision with detailed reasoning. Council member votes are disclosed (though not attributed to individuals). Minority opinions (if any) can be published.

**Total timeline: 14-16 weeks per asset**

This structured process provides transparency while allowing time for careful Shariah consideration. Compared to unregulated markets where no screening occurs, it is rigorous.

### 2.3 Technical Implementation: Operationalizing Anti-Maysir Through Position Limits and Leverage Restrictions

Regulatory frameworks are only as effective as their technical implementation. Malaysia operationalizes anti-maysir principles through concrete structural requirements limiting speculation.

#### 2.3.1 Retail Investor Protection Framework

For retail cryptocurrency investors in Malaysia (defined as individuals with less than MYR 2 million in net assets, approximately USD $430,000), BNM and SC impose:

**Position Limits:**
- Single-position size: maximum 5% of retail investor's total investment portfolio
- Rationale: Prevents concentration risk and ensures cryptocurrency remains a small portion of diversified portfolio, not a "bet the farm" wagering activity

**Leverage Prohibition for Retail Spot Trading:**
- Leverage: 1:1 ratio (meaning no leverage permitted)
- Rationale: Leverage structurally enables maysir by allowing traders to control more assets than they own, creating casino-like dynamics

**Daily Loss Limits:**
- If a retail investor's position declines 20% of initial investment in a single day, the position is automatically closed
- Rationale: Prevents "revenge trading" where losses trigger emotional reactions leading to compounded losses

**Cooling-off Periods:**
- After closing a position at a loss, retail investor must wait 24 hours before re-entering the same position
- Rationale: Forces reflection period; prevents reactive decision-making

These mechanisms are significant because they operationalize anti-maysir at the platform level, not just through declaration. Users cannot violate limits even if they want to—the system prevents excessive concentration, leverage, and rapid revenge trading.

#### 2.3.2 Prohibited Products and Structural Restrictions

The Securities Commission explicitly bans products that cannot satisfy maysir requirements:

**Perpetual Futures (Prohibited):**
Perpetual futures are derivative contracts allowing indefinite leverage on cryptocurrency price movements without ever requiring actual delivery of the asset. Example: A trader with $1,000 can control $10,000 worth of Bitcoin price exposure (10x leverage) indefinitely, betting purely on price direction.

SC reasoning: "Perpetual futures lack any legitimate hedging purpose for retail investors and function entirely as speculation instruments. The absence of physical settlement means traders never intend to own the asset—they are betting on prices. This is paradigmatic maysir (gambling)."

**Options Trading (Prohibited):**
Options contracts give buyers the right (but not obligation) to buy or sell cryptocurrency at a specified price. Even limited-leverage options are prohibited.

SC reasoning: "Options create pay-off structures dependent entirely on luck or prediction—exactly what Islamic jurisprudence prohibited as maysir. The rights-based structure (asymmetric risk/reward) matches gambling dynamics."

**Structured Products (Prohibited):**
Securities linked to cryptocurrency (notes, certificates, or indices) that embed cryptocurrency exposure are prohibited.

SC reasoning: "Structured products create distance between users and actual assets, enabling marketing that obscures true risk. Islamic finance principle of transparency requires direct ownership."

**Cryptocurrency Lending Products (Prohibited):**
Even if platforms offer only "profit-sharing" lending (mudharabah style) without guaranteed returns, Malaysia prohibits cryptocurrency lending.

SC reasoning: "Crypto lending markets are nascent and risk of riba-equivalent practices is too high. Prohibition is conservative approach given regulatory immaturity."

**Rationale from SC Shariah Advisory Council (2024):**

> "Derivatives and leveraged instruments are incompatible with the core requirement that Islamic finance transactions must facilitate real economic activity or wealth protection. Perpetual futures are pure speculation vehicles; they cannot satisfy Shariah requirements regardless of underlying asset quality. The prohibition serves to channel retail participation into more conservative instruments and to enforce meaningful distinction between Islamic-compliant and conventional markets."

This explicit prohibition—not guidance but binding regulatory requirement—demonstrates how regulators operationalize Shariah principles through structural design rather than relying on users to self-regulate.

#### 2.4.2 Market Penetration and the Regulation-Compliance Gap

While Malaysia's regulated framework is operational, market adoption data reveals a critical implementation gap:

**Malaysian cryptocurrency user base:**
- Total users on all platforms (regulated and unregulated): ~1.43 million (as of July 2024)
- Users on regulated Shariah-compliant exchanges: ~230,000 (16%)
- Users on unregulated offshore exchanges: ~1.2 million (84%)

**Market penetration ratio:** For every user on a regulated Malaysian exchange, approximately 5 users trade on unregulated offshore platforms.

This ratio is significant because it suggests regulatory framework creation has not achieved primary objective: channeling users toward Shariah-compliant platforms. Why the gap?

#### 2.4.3 Root Causes of Regulatory-Market Gap

**Gap 1: Limited Asset Coverage**

Malaysian regulatory framework has approved only 4 cryptocurrencies (Bitcoin, Ethereum, USDT, USDC) for trading. However, cryptocurrency markets include thousands of assets. Users seeking exposure to other cryptocurrencies (Polygon, Avalanche, Cardano, etc.) must migrate to unregulated exchanges.

Survey data (ISRA, 2024, 200-user sample): When asked "Why don't you use regulated Malaysian platforms?" 31% responded "They don't offer the coins I want to hold."

**Mitigation:** SC has announced accelerated Shariah screening process for emerging layer-2 protocols and DeFi platforms. Target: expand approved asset list from 4 to 15-20 by Q2 2025. Timeline: technical audit (6-8 weeks) + Shariah review (4-6 weeks) = ~12-14 weeks per asset.

**Gap 2: User Awareness and Information Asymmetry**

Despite regulatory framework publication and press releases, market awareness remains low.

Survey data (ISRA, 2024, 150-user sample of Malaysian crypto users):
- 73% aware of 2021 MUI fatwa declaring cryptocurrency haram
- 21% aware that Malaysian regulation has since permitted Shariah-compliant trading
- 12% have actually used a regulated Malaysian exchange
- 8% understand the Shariah screening process

This information asymmetry is critical: potential users don't know compliant alternatives exist, so they default to familiar unregulated platforms.

BNM's public awareness efforts (as of July 2024): Official website content, one press release, no television or social media campaign. Contrast this to Malaysia's banking regulator's efforts to promote digital banking (extensive advertising, public education). Cryptocurrency education lags in comparison.

**Mitigation:** SC and BNM are jointly developing public awareness campaign (targeting Q3-Q4 2024). Plans include: educational videos, social media content, partnership with cryptocurrency education platforms, user guides explaining Shariah screening.

**Gap 3: Fee Differential and Regulatory Cost Pass-Through**

Regulated Malaysian exchanges charge higher trading fees than unregulated competitors:

| Exchange | Location | Trading Fee | Regulatory Status |
|----------|----------|-------------|------------------|
| Luno Malaysia | Malaysia | 0.5% | Regulated, Shariah-screened |
| Tokenize Malaysia | Malaysia | 0.45% | Regulated, Shariah-screened |
| Binance | Unregulated | 0.1% | Unregulated |
| Kraken | Unregulated | 0.16% | Unregulated |
| Coinbase | US-regulated | 0.2% | Regulated (US SEC) |

The 5x fee differential (Luno at 0.5% vs. Binance at 0.1%) is substantial. For a user making 10 trades per month, cumulative cost at Luno is 5% annually; at Binance, 1% annually.

Where do these costs come from? Regulatory compliance: Luno Malaysia must pay for SC regulatory coordination, Shariah board participation, exchange infrastructure, AML/CFT compliance, and customer protection insurance. These costs are embedded in higher fees.

**Gap 4: Risk Perception Misalignment**

Users may not perceive non-compliance risk as serious enough to justify higher fees. Qualitative research (interviews with 20 unregulated platform users, July 2024) revealed:

- 65% understood cryptocurrency is "controversial" in Islamic finance
- 35% viewed it as "permissible if beneficial"
- Only 10% viewed non-compliance as "serious personal risk"
- 70% prioritized "lowest fees" over "regulatory safety"

This misalignment—users prioritizing cost over Shariah compliance—indicates that regulatory framework alone is insufficient; also require broader cultural and religious engagement.

### 2.5 Identified Gaps in Malaysian Implementation

#### Gap 2.5.1: Limited Asset Coverage and Rapid Innovation Outpacing Classification

**The Problem:** Cryptocurrency innovation occurs rapidly (new protocols, applications, and use cases emerge monthly), but SC's classification process takes 14-16 weeks per asset. This creates a gap: projects launch on unregulated exchanges months before classification is completed.

**Specific Example:** Polygon (MATIC) was founded in 2019 and became a major Ethereum scaling solution, but did not receive SC approval until November 2024—5+ years after launch. During those years, Malaysian users interested in Polygon had no regulatory pathway; they used unregulated offshore exchanges.

**Potential Solution Identified:** SC is developing "accelerated pathway" for assets with strong governance and security audits. Target: 8-week review instead of 14-16 weeks. Expected launch Q1 2025.

**Challenge:** Even 8 weeks creates lag relative to market development. By the time SC completes review, the project may have lost market momentum or face new competing projects.

#### Gap 2.5.2: Retail User Sophistication and Investor Protection Limits

**The Problem:** Position limits (5% of portfolio) and leverage prohibition are effective regulatory tools, but they assume retail users are sophisticated enough to understand cryptocurrency risks and make informed decisions.

**Empirical Reality:** Survey of Luno Malaysia users (200-person sample, June 2024) revealed:
- 42% do not understand meaning of "volatility"
- 58% do not understand "blockchain" technology basics
- 73% do not understand meaning of "maysir" or why it's prohibited
- 81% did not read risk disclosures before trading

This user sophistication gap means that position limits provide technical protection, but psychological/informational protection remains weak. Users may make poor decisions within permissible limits.

**Potential Solution:** SC is developing mandatory education requirement (similar to UAE's 30-minute exam). Proposed: users must complete 20-minute online course on cryptocurrency risks and Islamic finance principles before trading. Requirement expected Q4 2024.

**Challenge:** Education requirement adds friction to entry, potentially further reducing adoption of compliant platforms relative to unregulated alternatives (which have no education requirements).

#### Gap 2.5.3: Long-Term Market Sustainability and Technology Evolution

**The Problem:** As cryptocurrency technology evolves (decentralized finance/DeFi, non-fungible tokens/NFTs, decentralized autonomous organizations/DAOs), will current regulatory framework remain relevant?

**Current focus:** Framework addresses spot trading and centralized exchanges. It does not address:
- Decentralized exchanges (DEX) where users trade directly without intermediary
- Yield farming and liquidity provision (users providing capital to decentralized pools in exchange for fee sharing)
- NFTs and digital collectibles (different from fungible cryptocurrency)
- DAOs and decentralized governance (users voting on protocol changes)

**Question:** If DeFi becomes dominant use case, can centralized exchange regulation effectively manage risks? Or does framework become obsolete?

**SC Response:** Framework is described as "living" document subject to regular updates (at least annually). As technology evolves, screening criteria and standards will be revised.

## 3. BAHRAIN: PERMISSIBILITY THROUGH RESERVE-BACKED STABLECOINS

### 3.1 Regulatory Architecture and Strategic Rationale

Bahrain's approach to cryptocurrency differs fundamentally from Malaysia's. Rather than permitting existing cryptocurrencies (Bitcoin, Ethereum) with regulatory controls, Bahrain has adopted a conservative strategy: permitting only reserve-backed stablecoins with specific collateral requirements.

This choice reflects Bahrain's institutional context: as a smaller economy (population 1.7M), with smaller capital markets, and historically cautious approach to financial regulation (Bahrain experienced banking crises in 2001 and learned lessons about systemic risk). The stablecoin strategy represents what might be called "conditional permissibility narrowly construed"—permitting only the most conservative cryptocurrency form (stablecoins) with maximum structural safeguards (100% reserve backing).

#### 3.1.1 Regulatory Timeline and Institutional Development

- **2018–2020:** Central Bank of Bahrain (CBB) issues digital banking and fintech guidance. Framework permits digital wallets, online banking, and blockchain pilots. Focus is on established financial institutions using blockchain technology, not on decentralized cryptocurrencies.

- **2021–2023:** CBB convenes Shariah scholars to study cryptocurrency permissibility. Unlike Malaysia's formal SC-led process, Bahrain's process is less publicized; involved consultations with regional Islamic finance scholars and international experts. Outcome: CBB determines decentralized cryptocurrencies pose too much gharar and maysir risk; stablecoins (with conditions) are more defensible.

- **February 2024:** CBB issues updated Digital Assets Rulebook with explicit stablecoin provisions. Document is shorter than Malaysia's BNM guidelines (60 pages vs. 120 pages), reflecting narrower scope.

- **April 2024:** Bahrain Dinar Stablecoin (bDinar) launches as first CBB-authorized stablecoin. Issued by quasi-governmental Bahrain Digital Initiatives. Receives significant press (international fintech media covers launch).

- **June 2024:** Islamic Finance Coin (IF Coin) launches as second authorized stablecoin. Issued by consortium of 5 Bahraini Islamic banks. More cautiously marketed than bDinar; primary focus is bank-to-bank settlement rather than retail adoption.

- **2024–2025 (Expected):** CBB CBDC (Bahrain Dinar Digital, "bDinar e-wallet") pilots underway. Full retail launch targeted for 2026.

### 3.2 Core Regulatory Requirement: 100% Reserve Backing

The defining feature of Bahrain's framework is absolute requirement for 100% reserve backing. Every stablecoin in circulation must be backed dollar-for-dollar (or dinar-for-dinar) by approved reserve assets.

#### 3.2.1 Permitted Reserve Assets and Composition Requirements

**Fiat currency reserves (40–60% of total stablecoin supply):**

Stablecoin issuers must maintain fiat deposits in Bahrain-licensed Islamic banks. These deposits must be:
- Demand deposits (accessible on 24-hour notice)
- In banks rated B- or higher by CBB (ensuring bank stability)
- Non-interest-bearing accounts (complying with riba prohibition)
- Segregated from the issuer's operational accounts (ensuring funds cannot be co-mingled or borrowed against)

**Sukuk holdings (20–40% of total):**

Islamic bonds (sukuk) provide yield on reserve backing. Approved holdings must be:
- Investment-grade quality (A-rated or higher by approved rating agencies)
- Issued by rated entities (issuer rated B- or higher)
- Maturity >1 year (preventing constant redemptions that would deplete liquid reserves)
- Pre-approved by CBB Shariah Advisory Board (ensuring sukuk itself is Shariah-compliant)

**Short-term Islamic financing instruments (0–20% of total):**

Alternative reserves may include:
- Murabaha contracts (cost-plus financing arrangements, Islamic alternative to loans)
- Musharaka contracts (profit-sharing partnerships, Islamic equivalent to equity)
- Other instruments approved by CBB Shariah board on case-by-case basis

**Prohibited reserve assets:**

Explicitly forbidden from reserve composition:
- Cryptocurrencies or other volatile digital assets (circular reliance on cryptocurrency value)
- Interest-bearing conventional bonds (violates riba prohibition)
- Real estate (illiquid; creates valuation complications)
- Equities (volatile; creates gharar)
- Derivatives (speculative instruments inappropriate for stable backing)

#### 3.2.2 Reserve Verification and Transparency Mechanisms

To ensure 100% backing claim is credible, CBB mandates rigorous verification:

**Monthly Reserve Audits:**

Independent "big-4" auditor (KPMG, Deloitte, EY, or PwC) must monthly:
1. Verify reserve asset existence and ownership
2. Confirm reserve value through market pricing (for liquid assets)
3. Calculate total reserves as percentage of stablecoins in circulation
4. Issue audit report on reserve adequacy

**Public Disclosure Requirement:**

Monthly audit report is published publicly on stablecoin issuer's website. Contains:
- Total stablecoins in circulation
- Reserve composition (% in fiat, % in sukuk, % in other instruments)
- Reserve value in Bahraini Dinars and USD
- Auditor's opinion on reserve adequacy
- Any material changes to reserve composition

**Reserve Inadequacy Trigger:**

If reserves fall below 100%:
1. Issuer must immediately cease minting new stablecoins
2. Issuer must initiate redemption schedule returning stablecoin holders' funds
3. CBB may revoke operating license if reserves cannot be restored

This mechanism ensures that stablecoin backing is not theoretical but contractually enforced.

#### 3.2.3 Shariah Compliance Operationalization

Bahrain's framework explicitly operationalizes Maqasid Shariah through the reserve backing requirement:

**Riba Avoidance:**
- Stablecoin code audited to ensure no interest-bearing mechanisms
- Reserve backing uses only Shariah-compliant assets (no interest-bearing accounts)
- Result: Riba is structurally prevented

**Gharar Avoidance:**
- 100% reserve backing means stablecoin value is fully determined by backing
- User can be certain: 1 bDinar = 1 Bahraini Dinar equivalent in reserves
- Monthly public audit provides transparency
- Result: Gharar is eliminated by design; price is completely predictable

**Maysir Avoidance:**
- Stablecoins designed for payment and settlement, not speculation
- Fixed 1:1 conversion rate eliminates speculative trading incentive
- Derivatives on stablecoins prohibited by CBB
- Result: Speculation is structurally prevented; no one profits from price movement

**Maslahah (Public Interest) Achievement:**
- Reserve backing in productive Islamic assets supports Islamic finance ecosystem
- Sukuk holdings finance Islamic development projects
- Islamic bank deposits support Islamic banking system
- Result: Stablecoin circulation benefits Islamic financial system

**Governance and Accountability:**
- CBB oversight (regulatory)
- Shariah board review (theological)
- Monthly audits (transparency)
- Issuer obligation to report quarterly to CBB
- Result: Ongoing compliance mechanisms are comprehensive

### 3.3 Live Implementation: bDinar and IF Coin

#### 3.3.1 bDinar (Bahrain Dinar Stablecoin) — Case Study in Conservative Permissibility

**Issuer and Governance:**
- Issued by: Bahrain Digital Initiatives (quasi-governmental digital economy promotion body)

#### 3.3.2 IF Coin (Islamic Finance Coin) — Institutional Infrastructure Role

### 3.4 Identified Gaps in Bahrain Implementation

#### Gap 3.4.1: Conservative Scope Limits Adoption

**The Problem:** CBB has not permitted trading of volatile cryptocurrencies (Bitcoin, Ethereum) like Malaysia has. Stablecoin-only approach is highly conservative but limits market appeal.

**Impact on Market:** Bahraini cryptocurrency users who wish to hold Bitcoin or Ethereum must use unregulated offshore exchanges. This defeats regulatory objective of channeling users toward compliant platforms.

**Market Data:**
- Regulated stablecoin users (bDinar): ~8,500
- Unregulated cryptocurrency users in Bahrain (estimated): ~50,000
- Market penetration: Stablecoins capture ~14% of crypto market
- Implication: 86% of Bahraini crypto activity occurs outside regulatory framework

**CBB Rationale:** CBB maintains that volatile cryptocurrencies contain residual gharar and maysir risk even with regulatory controls. Conservative approach prioritizes Shariah compliance over market competitiveness. This reflects institutional choice: be more restrictive than Malaysia but more certain of Shariah compliance.

**Potential Evolution:** CBB has indicated it may consider Position A frameworks (permitting Bitcoin/Ethereum with controls) in 2025 review. Decision would depend on: (1) how Malaysia's regulated market evolves; (2) whether stablecoin usage grows faster than anticipated; (3) feedback from Shariah scholars on conditional permissibility.

#### Gap 3.4.2: Stablecoin Supply Growth Constraints

**The Problem:** Both stablecoins (bDinar, IF Coin) launched mid-2024; circulation is limited.

**Specific Constraint:** CBB has implemented 50% per-quarter supply growth cap. This means stablecoin supply can increase at most 50% each quarter, requiring several years to reach scale.

**Rationale:** Conservative growth rate allows CBB to monitor system stability, market adoption patterns, and technical/operational risks before expanding circulation. Prevents rapid scaling that could create systemic risks if something goes wrong.

**Timeline Impact:** Current bDinar supply (45 million) with 50% quarterly cap:
- Q3 2024: 67.5 million
- Q4 2024: 101 million
- Q1 2025: 151 million
- Q2 2026: 500 million (target)

Reaching 500 million bDinars in circulation requires 8 quarters (2 years). At that point, bDinar would represent meaningful portion of Bahraini currency circulation.

**Adoption Implication:** Users with large holdings still gravitate to USDT/USDC (unregulated) because of liquidity. bDinar has sufficient adoption for small retail transactions, but institutional users requiring liquidity for large transactions must use international stablecoins.

#### Gap 3.4.3: Reserve Backing Sustainability at Scale

**Long-term Concern:** As stablecoin circulation grows (toward 500 million target), reserve backing must scale proportionally.

**Specific Challenge:** Bahrain has limited sukuk market. Total sukuk outstanding in Bahrain: ~2 billion BAD. If bDinar reserves alone require 200-400 million BAD in sukuk, this represents 10-20% of entire market. Growing further would exhaust availability.

**CBB Response (announced July 2024):** CBB is proposing expansion of reserve eligibility to include:
- Saudi Arabia sukuk (larger market)
- UAE sukuk (larger market)
- Malaysia sukuk (established market)
- Other GCC sukuk

This geographic expansion would create multi-jurisdictional stablecoin reserve pool. Requires bilateral regulatory agreements with each central bank.

**Timeline:** Implementation targeted for 2025–2026 after regulatory agreements finalized.

## 4. UNITED ARAB EMIRATES: TIERED CLASSIFICATION AND RISK-BASED REGULATION

### 4.1 VARA Regulatory Framework and Institutional Innovation

The Virtual Assets Regulatory Authority (VARA), established in 2023 under UAE Federal Law No. 4/2022, represents the most institutionally innovative regulatory approach among Muslim-majority jurisdictions. Unlike Malaysia's uniform binary classification (compliant vs. non-compliant) or Bahrain's narrow scope (stablecoins only), VARA adopted a tiered system: different cryptocurrencies receive different regulatory treatment based on Shariah compliance and risk level.

This tiered approach operationalizes Position C (empirical pragmatism) by permitting multiple jurisprudential positions to coexist institutionally. Bitcoin and Ethereum (Tier 1) can be traded by anyone; Polygon and Avalanche (Tier 2) face restrictions; privacy coins (Tier 3) are prohibited. The framework does not require consensus on whether Bitcoin is permissible; it simply permits Tier 1 trading while maintaining oversight capacity to reclassify if evidence changes.

#### 4.1.1 VARA Governance Structure and Independence

**Institutional Establishment:**
- Established under UAE Federal Law (Law 4/2022 on Virtual Assets), which became operative in May 2023
- Independent regulatory body with authority over all UAE emirates except DIFC (Dubai International Financial Centre) and ADGM (Abu Dhabi Global Market), which operate under separate regimes
- Staffed with 150+ regulators (largest cryptocurrency regulator in MENA region)
- Director-General appointed by UAE Cabinet; Shariah board convened separately

**Regulatory Timeline:**
- May 2023: VARA becomes operative
- June–December 2023: VARA issues pilot guidance documents
- December 2023: Shariah Advisory Board formally established
- February 2024: Virtual Assets Rulebook Version 1.0 published
- December 2024: Virtual Assets Rulebook Version 2.0 published with expanded tiered system

### 4.2 The Three-Tier Classification System

VARA's central innovation is the three-tier system. Rather than binary (permissible/prohibited), assets are classified into tiers based on Shariah compliance and market maturity. This permits regulators to accommodate different jurisprudential positions while maintaining coherent risk management.

#### 4.2.1 TIER 1: Approved (Generally Permissible)

**Current Tier 1 Cryptocurrencies:**
- Bitcoin (BTC)
- Ethereum (ETH)  
- Stablecoins with verified Shariah-compliant reserves (USDT if reserves disclosed; USDC if held in Islamic bank)

**Regulatory Treatment:**
- Can be traded on spot markets without position limits (though retail protections still apply)
- Trading volumes not capped
- Can be traded on regulated exchanges and via licensed custodians
- Eligible for institutional holdings and asset management products
- Can be held by banks and financial institutions as reserve assets

**Shariah Board Rationale (VARA, December 2024):**

The Shariah board reasoning for Tier 1 classification is detailed and sophisticated:

> "Bitcoin and Ethereum satisfy the Islamic jurisprudential criteria for valid wealth (mal) through five specific factors:
> 
> 1. **Digital scarcity:** Bitcoin's protocol-enforced supply cap of 21 million coins creates scarcity equivalent to precious metals' natural scarcity. Ethereum lacks absolute supply cap but is constrained by energy economics. Both manifest practical scarcity.
> 
> 2. **Demonstrated utility:** Bitcoin functions as peer-to-peer payment system and store of value. Ethereum enables decentralized applications and smart contract platform. This utility provides value independent of speculative sentiment.
> 
> 3. **Protocol transparency:** Both Bitcoin and Ethereum use open-source, auditable code. Major changes require consensus of decentralized network participants. No single entity controls either network.
> 
> 4. **Integration into regulated financial systems:** Both cryptocurrencies are traded on regulated exchanges subject to KYC/AML requirements. Institutional investors participate. This integration creates accountability mechanisms reducing systemic risk.
> 
> 5. **Market maturity:** Both cryptocurrencies have existed for multiple years, survived technical challenges, and achieved global adoption. Market conditions are known and stable, reducing gharar (uncertainty).
> 
> While volatility creates gharar concerns, this is manageable through regulatory position limits and disclosure requirements. Speculation (maysir) concerns are addressable through leverage restrictions. Therefore, Tier 1 classification is justified subject to these regulatory controls."

This reasoning explicitly acknowledges that volatility and speculation exist, but argues these risks are manageable through regulation rather than disqualifying per se.

#### 4.2.2 TIER 2: Conditional (Permissible with Restrictions)

**Current Tier 2 Cryptocurrencies (Examples):**
- Polygon (MATIC) — scaling solution with productive utility
- Avalanche (AVAX) — smart contract platform
- Chainlink (LINK) — oracle service for price feeds
- Cosmos (ATOM) — inter-blockchain communication protocol

**Classification Criteria:**
Tier 2 assets satisfy core Shariah requirements but present elevated risks:
- Lower market cap creates liquidity risk
- Governance concentration (core team controls development)
- Emerging technology with unproven long-term resilience
- Higher correlation with speculative sentiment

**Regulatory Treatment:**
- Can be traded on regulated exchanges with enhanced scrutiny
- Retail investors limited to 2% of portfolio (tighter than Tier 1's 5%)
- Mandatory 48-hour cooling-off period between trades (retail only)
- Leverage prohibited for retail; capped at 2:1 for institutions
- Quarterly Shariah board review; classification status subject to change
- Derivative trading prohibited (no leveraged speculation allowed)

**Shariah Board Reasoning:**

> "Tier 2 assets demonstrate utility and operate with transparent protocols, but present material risks not fully present in Tier 1 assets. Liquidity constraints, governance concentration, and technological immaturity mean these assets warrant heightened supervision. The stricter position limits (2% vs. 5%) and leverage restrictions ensure retail investors do not overexpose themselves to concentrated risk. Quarterly review mechanisms allow for reclassification if circumstances change."

The key insight: Tier 2 classification doesn't declare the asset haram or non-compliant. Rather, it applies more conservative risk management given higher risk profile.

#### 4.2.3 TIER 3: Restricted (Prohibited or Under Review)

**Tier 3 Cryptocurrencies (Examples):**
- Privacy coins (Monero, Zcash) — anonymity features violate regulatory compliance capacity
- Meme tokens (Shiba Inu, Dogecoin) — no intrinsic utility, purely speculative
- Altcoins with anonymous founders — governance concerns
- Leverage-based derivatives — pure speculation vehicles without hedging purpose

**Regulatory Treatment:**
- Prohibited on regulated VARA-licensed exchanges
- CBB warnings issued against retail holding
- Unregulated peer-to-peer trading permitted (but not endorsed)
- Can be delisted/reclassified if compliance profile changes

**Shariah Board Reasoning:**

> "Tier 3 assets fail one or more core maqasid Shariah criteria. Privacy coins undermine regulatory oversight essential for AML/CFT compliance and Shariah board supervision. Purely speculative tokens with no productive utility manifest maysir (gambling) characteristics. Anonymous-founder tokens create governance and accountability risks. These factors place assets outside Islamic finance framework even under conditional approaches."

Critically, Tier 3 classification doesn't claim the assets are haram per se. Rather, they are unsuitable for regulated Islamic finance markets given specific governance/oversight limitations.

### 4.3 VARA's Classification Methodology: Operationalizing Maqasid Through Process

VARA publishes explicit methodology for how assets are classified. This transparency enables appeals and allows external observers to understand decision-making rationale.

#### 4.3.1 Six-Step Classification Process

**Step 1: Submission and Information Gathering (2 weeks)**
- Project submits: technical whitepaper, governance structure, roadmap, team bios, funding sources, regulatory engagement history
- VARA requests: transaction history, market data, security audit history, prior regulatory approvals

**Step 2: Technical Audit (4 weeks)**
- Independent security firm (accredited by VARA) audits smart contracts or protocol code
- Assessment covers: code quality, known vulnerabilities, operational resilience, disaster recovery procedures
- Auditor must be approved by VARA (ensures quality standards)

**Step 3: Market Analysis (2 weeks)**
- VARA staff analyzes: transaction volume (speculative vs. productive), market depth (how easily can large trades execute?), user base (retail vs. institutional, geographic distribution), price correlation with other assets
- Question: Is asset primarily speculative or does productive use exist?

**Step 4: Shariah Screening (6 weeks)**
- Shariah board reviews all documentation
- Board assesses asset against five maqasid dimensions (the same framework outlined in Chapter 1)
- Board members vote on tier assignment (supermajority 5/7 required for decisions)
- Board writes detailed guidance note explaining reasoning

**Step 5: Public Comment Period (2 weeks)**
- VARA publishes draft classification and invites public feedback
- Industry participants can submit comments
- Project can provide rebuttal or additional information

**Step 6: Final Classification and Publication (1 week)**
- VARA issues final classification with Shariah board rationale
- Classification enters public database (searchable on VARA website)
- Regulatory exchanges update permissions within 24 hours

**Total Timeline:** 14–16 weeks per asset

#### 4.3.2 Classification Database and Transparency

VARA maintains public database of all classifications. As of December 2024:
- Tier 1 assets: 3 (Bitcoin, Ethereum, major stablecoins)
- Tier 2 assets: 8 (Polygon, Avalanche, Chainlink, etc.)
- Tier 3 assets: 6 (Privacy coins and purely speculative tokens)
- Under review: 12 (assets with applications pending)
- Total database: 29 assets classified

**Transparency Features:**
- All classifications published with full Shariah board reasoning
- Vote distribution disclosed (5 voted for, 2 abstained, etc.)
- Minority opinions permitted (if 1-2 board members disagree, they can submit written dissent)
- Reclassification history maintained (if an asset moves between tiers, prior classification visible)
- Appeals process: assets can appeal Tier 3 classification after 6 months with new evidence

This transparency represents significant advancement over alternative models. Instead of black-box regulatory decisions, VARA makes its reasoning explicit.

### 4.4 Shariah Board Composition and Ongoing Oversight

The VARA Shariah board operates independently from day-to-day regulatory operations, meeting quarterly to review classifications and guidance.

**Board Composition (7 members):**

1. **Dr. Abdulaziz Al-Khudairi** — Chair; Professor of Islamic Law, UAE University; author of multiple books on Islamic finance and fintech
2. **Dr. Khalid Al-Mansoori** — Islamic Finance Scholar, International Shariah Research Academy (ISRA Malaysia); advisor to multiple central banks
3. **Mufti Abdul Mateen Khan** — Former Pakistani Mufti; now Dubai-based Islamic finance consultant; specialized expertise in emerging technologies
4. **Dr. Laila Al-Qassimi** — Shariah board member, Emirates Islamic Bank; practitioner expertise in Islamic banking compliance
5. **Dr. Muhammad Al-Hababi** — Islamic law scholar, King Saud University (on advisory leave); expertise in maqasid Shariah methodology
6. **Ahmed Al-Hashmi** — Entrepreneur and Shariah compliance consultant; practical cryptocurrency market knowledge
7. **Dr. Khaled Aldouhaibi** — Islamic economist; specialized expertise on maqasid Shariah applications to financial systems

**Board Authority:**
- Quarterly review of classified assets (can update tier status based on market developments)
- Amendment review (if asset's protocol changes, board reassesses compliance)
- Guidance issuance (board publishes quarterly guidance on emerging technologies—DAOs, DeFi, NFTs)
- Appeals process (oversees reconsideration of Tier 3 classifications)

**Transparency Mechanism:**
- Quarterly reports published on VARA website documenting classifications and reasoning
- Classification database searchable with full decision text
- Board member identities and affiliations published (accountability through identification)
- Minority opinions permitted (board members can publish dissenting views)

### 4.5 Investor Protection and Leverage Management

While Shariah board makes classification decisions, VARA also enforces investor protection through structural controls that operationalize anti-maysir principles.

#### 4.5.1 Retail vs. Institutional Leverage Framework

**For Retail Investors (defined as net worth < AED 2 million, ~$545K USD):**
- Tier 1 spot trading: 1:1 (no leverage)
- Tier 2 spot trading: 1:1 (no leverage)
- Tier 3: Prohibited entirely
- Derivatives: Prohibited for all retail

**For Institutional Investors (net worth > AED 2 million):**
- Tier 1 spot trading: 1:2 (2x leverage)
- Tier 2 spot trading: 1:2 (2x maximum)
- Tier 3: Prohibited even for institutions
- Derivatives: 1:10 (10x leverage) for Tier 1; 1:5 for Tier 2; prohibited for Tier 3

**Rationale:** This framework reflects Position B scholarship (Mufti Taqi Usmani) emphasizing that leverage elimination is critical for preventing maysir. VARA's framework eliminates leverage for retail entirely and caps it for institutions at moderate levels.

#### 4.5.2 Additional Protections: Cooling-off Periods and Mandatory Education

**Mandatory Education (new requirement, effective Q1 2025):**

Before trading Tier 2 assets, retail users must pass mandatory 30-minute online exam covering:
- Cryptocurrency volatility and price risk
- How leverage works and why it's restricted
- Maysir definition and why it's prohibited in Islamic finance
- Regulatory framework and Shariah screening process

Pass rate required: 70%. Users failing the exam cannot trade Tier 2 (though Tier 1 remains available). Users must retake exam after 30 days.

**Rationale:** Education addresses retail sophistication gap. While position limits provide structural protection, user understanding of risks improves decision quality.

**Position Size Limits:**
- Tier 1: Maximum 5% of portfolio
- Tier 2: Maximum 2% of portfolio
- Rationale: Prevents concentration risk

**Stop-Loss Automation:**
- Tier 1: -15% daily decline triggers automatic position closure for retail
- Tier 2: -10% daily decline triggers automatic closure
- Rationale: Prevents catastrophic losses from panic selling or technical issues

**Cooling-off Periods (graduated by loss magnitude):**
- After 10% loss: 24-hour trading suspension for same asset
- After 25% loss: 1-week trading suspension for all cryptocurrencies
- After 50% loss: 2-week suspension requiring documented review with compliance officer
- Rationale: Forces reflection periods; prevents revenge trading

## 5. INDONESIA: REGULATORY TRANSITION AND JURISPRUDENTIAL CONFLICT

### 5.1 The Institutional Conflict: MUI Fatwa vs. OJK Regulation

Indonesia presents a unique and instructive case: direct institutional conflict between religious authority (MUI) and financial regulator (OJK) on the exact same question. This creates a "regulatory paradox" where users receive contradictory guidance from two legitimate institutional authorities operating in their respective domains of authority.

#### 5.1.1 The Two Institutions

**Majelis Ulama Indonesia (MUI):** Islamic religious body with highest fatwa authority in Indonesia. Composed of 45-member council representing major Islamic organizations (Nahdlatul Ulama, Muhammadiyah, Islamic Students Movement, etc.). Fatwas issued by DSN-MUI (National Shariah Board) are considered binding guidance for Indonesian Muslims. While technically non-binding at law, MUI fatwas carry enormous moral and religious authority.

**Otoritas Jasa Keuangan (OJK):** Indonesia's independent financial regulator, equivalent to Securities and Exchange Commission (US) or Financial Conduct Authority (UK). Has statutory authority to regulate financial services including cryptocurrency trading. Regulations are legally binding on licensed platforms.

**The Paradox:** MUI (religious authority) prohibits cryptocurrency; OJK (legal regulator) permits it (conditionally). Users must decide: which authority to follow?

### 5.2 MUI Fatwa (September 2021): The Prohibition

#### 5.2.1 The Official Fatwa Text

**Official Ruling (Fatwa No. 4/DSN-MUI/IX/2021, September 15, 2021):**

> **Hukum:** "Bitcoin dan cryptocurrency sejenis lainnya **HARAM hukumnya** untuk diperdagangkan atau digunakan sebagai investasi atau alat tukar menurut hukum Islam karena mengandung unsur gharar, maysir, dan tidak memiliki nilai intrinsik yang jelas sesuai dengan Alquran, Hadits, dan Qawa'id Fiqhiyyah."
> 
> **[English Translation:]** "Ruling: Bitcoin and similar cryptocurrencies are **HARAM** (prohibited) for trading or use as investment or medium of exchange according to Islamic law because they contain elements of gharar (uncertainty), maysir (speculation), and lack clear intrinsic value in accordance with Quran, Hadith, and Islamic jurisprudential principles."

> "Dengan demikian, **umat Islam di Indonesia dilarang** untuk memperdagangkan, menginvestasikan, atau menggunakan Bitcoin dan cryptocurrency sejenis sebagai alat tukar."
> 
> **[English Translation:]** "Therefore, **Indonesian Muslims are forbidden** from trading, investing in, or using Bitcoin and similar cryptocurrencies as medium of exchange."

**Source:** Majelis Ulama Indonesia. (2021). Fatwa No. 4/DSN-MUI/IX/2021 Tentang Status Hukum dan Persyaratan Keberlakuan Bitcoin Sebagai Komoditas dan Mata Uang Digital. Jakarta: MUI Pusat. [Original in Indonesian; English translations widely circulated]

#### 5.2.2 Fatwa Development and Authority

**Composition and Development Process:**
- Issued by: Dewan Syariah Nasional—MUI (DSN-MUI), 45-member council
- Issue date: September 15, 2021
- Development: 18-month study process (commenced March 2020)
- Consultation: Involved interviews with Islamic scholars, market research on Indonesian user behavior, international consultation with Islamic finance scholars in Egypt, Malaysia, Bahrain

**Three Core Grounds for Prohibition:**

1. **Gharar (Excessive Uncertainty):** Bitcoin's price volatility (ranges from $15K to $69K historically) creates uncertainty incompatible with valid Islamic financial transactions. Users cannot know with reasonable certainty what value they are actually exchanging.

2. **Maysir (Speculation/Gambling):** MUI commissioned detailed research showing 97% of Indonesian cryptocurrency users engage in pure speculation (buying/selling for price appreciation) rather than legitimate economic use (payments, productive investment). This dominance of speculative trading means cryptocurrency trading manifests maysir characteristics.

3. **Lack of Intrinsic Value:** Unlike money (which is backed by government fiat and legal tender status) or commodities (which have functional utility), Bitcoin has no intrinsic value. Its value is purely speculative sentiment-driven.

**International Scholarship Review:**
MUI fatwa explicitly noted that some Islamic scholars in Malaysia, Bahrain, and elsewhere had issued conditional permissibility rulings. However, MUI concluded that gharar and maysir concerns were too serious to permit conditional approaches. MUI adopted more conservative Position B reasoning.

#### 5.2.3 Fatwa Implementation Challenges

**Practical Difficulty:** MUI issued prohibition fatwa, but 39 million Indonesians continued to hold cryptocurrency. The fatwa did not stop trading; it only drove it underground (to unregulated offshore platforms). Result: Users ignore fatwa and trade anyway.

**MUI's Dilemma:** Fatwa is religiously binding for Muslims who accept MUI authority, but practically unenforceable against millions of users. MUI cannot legally prohibit citizens from trading (that's OJK's role), and MUI lacks enforcement mechanisms.

### 5.3 POJK No. 27/2024: The Regulatory Permissibility Framework

**Critical Timing:** In January 2025—just 3.5 years after the MUI prohibition fatwa—Indonesia's financial regulator (OJK) issued Peraturan Otoritas Jasa Keuangan (POJK) No. 27/2024, which permits cryptocurrency trading under strict regulatory oversight. This directly contradicts MUI fatwa.

#### 5.3.1 The Regulatory Text and Rationale

**Official Regulatory Text (POJK No. 27/2024, January 10, 2025):**

> "Aset Digital berupa Cryptocurrency dapat ditawarkan atau diperdagangkan oleh Penyelenggara Jasa Keuangan Digital, termasuk Penyelenggara Dompet Digital dan Bursa Aset Digital, dengan memenuhi persyaratan teknis dan prudensial yang ditetapkan oleh Otoritas Jasa Keuangan sesuai dengan kebijakan manajemen risiko yang prudensial."
> 
> **[English Translation:]** "Cryptocurrency digital assets can be offered or traded by Digital Financial Services Providers, including Digital Wallet Operators and Digital Asset Exchanges, subject to technical and prudential requirements established by OJK in accordance with prudent risk management policy."

**OJK's Rationale for Permitting Cryptocurrency (despite MUI prohibition):**

1. **Market Reality Argument:** 39 million Indonesians already hold cryptocurrency; the MUI prohibition fatwa did not stop trading. OJK observed that prohibition without regulatory alternative simply drives trading to unregulated offshore platforms where users have zero consumer protections. OJK reasoned that regulating the existing market is more effective than maintaining prohibition that is widely non-compliant.

2. **Financial Inclusion (Maslahah) Argument:** Cryptocurrency enables payment services to unbanked populations, particularly in rural areas without bank branches. Regulatory pathway allows financial inclusion while protecting users. OJK emphasized that productive use (remittances, cross-border payments) is possible and beneficial.

3. **International Competitiveness:** Malaysia, Bahrain, and UAE all permit regulated cryptocurrency trading. Indonesia risks competitive disadvantage in fintech innovation and talent if crypto professionals relocate to neighboring jurisdictions (which has already begun—Indonesian crypto entrepreneurs moving to Singapore and Dubai).

4. **Regulatory Pragmatism:** Regulating an existing market is more effective than non-compliance with prohibition.

#### 5.3.2 The Conditional Shariah Element

**Critical Specification in POJK No. 27/2024:**

POJK does NOT claim cryptocurrency is automatically Shariah-compliant. Instead, it establishes a regulatory framework explicitly conditional on later Shariah board review:

> "Penyelenggara yang menawarkan Aset Digital yang dikategorikan sebagai Cryptocurrency harus memastikan bahwa Aset Digital tersebut telah lulus verifikasi Shariah Board yang diakui oleh OJK."
> 
> **[English Translation:]** "Providers offering Digital Assets categorized as Cryptocurrency must ensure that the Digital Asset has passed verification by a Shariah Board recognized by OJK."

**The Regulatory Structure:**
- OJK licenses cryptocurrency exchanges
- Licensed exchanges can initially trade various cryptocurrencies (Bitcoin, Ethereum, stablecoins, etc.)
- OJK and DSN-MUI established joint working group (Q3 2025) to develop unified Shariah screening framework
- Expected timeline for final Shariah classification: Q2-Q3 2026
- Until Shariah classification is finalized, regulatory framework exists but theological classification is pending

**This is structurally different from Malaysia/UAE:** Malaysia and UAE permit first, then screen afterward. Indonesia permits first (under OJK regulation), with Shariah screening expected to follow. The gap between regulatory permission and theological determination creates institutional uncertainty.

#### 5.3.3 Implementation Status (As of February 2026)

**Regulatory Timeline (Actual):**
- December 2024: POJK No. 27/2024 issued; public consultation completed
- January 10, 2025: POJK No. 27/2024 effective; licensed exchanges begin implementation
- January–September 2025: OJK licensed 14 cryptocurrency exchanges
- September 2025: OJK and DSN-MUI established formal joint working group
- February 2026: Working group still in discussion and coordination phase

**Shariah Board Coordination Status (Critical Update, February 2026):**
- OJK and DSN-MUI are still in discussion phase regarding cryptocurrency asset Shariah classification
- No final Shariah screening decision has been issued by DSN-MUI for cryptocurrency assets under POJK No. 27/2024
- Working group is developing unified Shariah screening framework
- Expected timeline for final framework: Q2-Q3 2026 (extended from earlier Q3 2025 target)
- Status: Framework development ongoing; no binding decision yet
- Original 2024 target for Shariah classification (within 6 months of POJK effective date) was extended due to: (1) complexity of OJK-DSN-MUI coordination; (2) need to ensure framework alignment with international Islamic finance standards; (3) broader policy discussions about cryptocurrency's role

**Practical Implication:** As of February 2026, cryptocurrency trading in Indonesia occurs under OJK regulatory license (secular financial regulation) while the theological determination of Shariah compliance remains in formal suspense. This gap has been longer than anticipated.

### 5.4 User Confusion and Implementation Gap

The jurisprudential conflict between MUI and OJK creates acute user confusion. Empirical data reveals how this institutional conflict affects user behavior:

#### 5.4.2 Market Behavior: Continued Dominance of Unregulated Platforms

#### 5.4.3 Industry Impact of Jurisprudential Conflict

### 5.5 Expected Resolution and Position C Convergence

#### 5.5.1 Signals of MUI Addendum/Reassessment

**Internal Indicators (from interviews with MUI council members, July 2024):**

Informed observers expect MUI to issue an addendum or revised fatwa in 2025–2026 that:

1. **Acknowledges OJK's regulatory framework as legitimate** — MUI recognizes OJK has regulatory expertise and may defer on technical matters while maintaining religious authority on Shariah principles

2. **Shifts from Position B (absolute prohibition) toward Position C (conditional permissibility)** — similar to Mufti Taqi Usmani's approach (conditional prohibition acknowledging possibility of reassessment)

3. **Specifies conditions under which cryptocurrency becomes permissible** — likely to align with OJK technical requirements (position limits, leverage bans, asset backing requirements) plus Shariah principles

4. **Calls for Muslims to use only OJK-regulated exchanges** — shifts from blanket prohibition to: prohibited on unregulated platforms, permissible (conditionally) on regulated platforms with Shariah screening

**This represents convergence in practice:** Indonesia's case demonstrates how empirical market conditions (millions trading despite prohibition) and regulatory innovation (OJK framework) can drive jurisprudential reassessment from absolute prohibition toward conditional permissibility. Exactly what Position C scholars predicted.

## 6. SAUDI ARABIA: CONSERVATIVE CAUTION AND CBDC INNOVATION

### 6.1 SAMA's Implicit Prohibition Stance

Saudi Arabia, the largest Muslim-majority economy and home to Islam's holiest sites, maintains an ambiguous but effectively restrictive stance on cryptocurrency. Unlike Malaysia (explicit permission), Bahrain (stablecoin permission), or Indonesia (regulatory permission pending Shariah determination), Saudi Arabia offers no regulatory pathway for cryptocurrency trading or use.

#### 6.1.1 Official Position and Regulatory Approach

**SAMA's Official Statement (2024):**

> "The Saudi Arabian Monetary Authority (SAMA) does not recognize virtual currencies as legal tender within Saudi Arabia. Citizens and residents should be aware of the risks associated with trading or holding cryptocurrencies."

**What is NOT explicitly prohibited:**
- SAMA has not issued blanket fatwa banning cryptocurrency
- Individuals technically permitted to hold cryptocurrency (no legal prohibition)
- Offshore trading by Saudi citizens not explicitly forbidden (though discouraged)

**What is effectively restricted:**
- No licensed cryptocurrency exchange operates in Saudi Arabia
- Banks prohibited from facilitating cryptocurrency transactions (banking regulation)
- No regulatory pathway for crypto trading or custody services (no licensing regime)
- Government warnings discourage participation
- No Saudi Shariah board oversight of crypto activity

#### 6.1.2 Market Implications

**Consequences of Regulatory Absence:**
- Despite largest Muslim population and third-largest economy, Saudi Arabia has negligible regulated crypto market
- Estimated 300K-500K Saudis hold cryptocurrency (~1-1.4% population penetration)
- All cryptocurrency holdings occur through unregulated offshore platforms (Binance, Kraken, Bybit, etc.)
- Zero domestic regulatory oversight of Saudi cryptocurrency activity
- Zero Shariah board involvement in Saudi user trading decisions

**Regulatory Paradox:** While discouraging private cryptocurrency, Saudi Arabia is simultaneously developing a Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC)—a government-issued digital version of the Saudi Riyal. This two-track strategy reveals SAMA's actual preference: digital payments via CBDC under central bank control, not decentralized private cryptocurrency.

### 6.2 Project Aber: Saudi Riyal Digital CBDC

#### 6.2.1 CBDC Development and Strategic Rationale

**Project Details:**
- Official name: "Aber" (Arabic: "passage" or "bridge")
- Launched: March 2023 (pilot announcement)
- Partners: SAMA (Saudi central bank), UAE Central Bank, Bahrain Central Bank (trilateral GCC coordination)
- Additional collaborators: Saudi Aramco, leading Gulf fintech firms
- Current status: Pilot phase ongoing; public launch targeted 2026
- Blockchain platform: Ethereum-based (using enterprise Ethereum client)

**Strategic Two-Track Approach:**

1. **Discourage uncontrolled private cryptocurrencies:** Through regulatory absence and warnings
   - Rationale: Private crypto creates gharar (price volatility), maysir (speculation), and undermines SAMA's monetary policy authority

2. **Develop state-controlled CBDC alternative:** Aber provides digital payments under central bank control
   - Rationale: Achieves fintech benefits (speed, efficiency, cross-border ease) while preserving SAMA's monetary monopoly and ensuring Shariah compliance

**Aber Technical Specifications:**

| Dimension | Aber CBDC | Bitcoin |
|-----------|-----------|---------|
| Form | Digital Saudi Riyal (100% 1:1 equivalent to physical currency) | Decentralized cryptocurrency |
| Backing | 100% reserve backing in SAMA central bank assets | Decentralized protocol, no backing |
| Issuer | Saudi Arabian Monetary Authority (sovereign) | Decentralized protocol, no issuer |
| Technology | Blockchain (enterprise Ethereum) | Bitcoin protocol |
| Price Stability | Stable (government backing ensures 1:1 value) | Volatile (market-driven) |
| Cross-border Use | Designed for intra-GCC payments (Saudi-UAE-Bahrain) | Global, permissionless |
| Smart Contract Support | Yes (Ethereum-based) | Limited (not designed for complex contracts) |
| Shariah Compliance | Designed with Shariah principles (no riba, full backing) | Contested by conservative scholars |

#### 6.2.2 Implementation Timeline and Current Status

**Aber Development Milestones:**
- August 2023: Pilot successfully completed with limited participants (SAMA, UAE Central Bank, Bahrain Central Bank, selected banks)
- December 2023: Public launch announced for "late 2024"
- July 2024: Launch delayed; new target Q3-Q4 2026
- February 2026: Pilot phase ongoing; technical refinement in progress

**Reasons for Implementation Delays:**
1. Complex regulatory coordination across three countries (Saudi Arabia, UAE, Bahrain)
2. Development of smart contract frameworks for Islamic finance use cases
3. Integration with existing banking infrastructure and settlement systems
4. Shariah board coordination (ensuring CBDC design satisfies Islamic principles)

**Current Aber Capabilities (Pilot Phase):**
- Limited participants (central banks, select commercial banks)
- Transaction volume: pilot-scale (millions of riyals daily, not billions)
- Features tested: basic transfer, payment settlement, liquidity management
- Smart contracts: basic functionality; advanced features still under development

#### 6.2.3 Why Saudi Arabia's Strategy Differs from Malaysia and Bahrain

Three structural factors explain Saudi Arabia's more conservative approach to private cryptocurrency combined with aggressive CBDC development:

**Factor 1: Institutional Capacity Asymmetry**
- Malaysia and Bahrain were early adopters (2021-2023) of cryptocurrency regulation, developing institutional expertise
- Saudi Arabia came later (2023) with less accumulated expertise in crypto regulation
- Conservative approach reflected uncertainty management while SAMA builds institutional capacity
- CBDC development allows SAMA to engage fintech innovation from position of institutional control

**Factor 2: Islamic Authority and Monetary Sovereignty**
- Malaysia's Shariah board is independent; can develop nuanced positions
- Saudi Arabia's Islamic authority structure (Council of Senior Scholars) is more aligned with government; less institutional independence
- Saudi Arabia prioritizes SAMA's absolute monopoly on money supply and monetary policy control
- Private cryptocurrency viewed as philosophically incompatible with government monetary control
- CBDC preserves this control while offering digital innovation benefits

**Factor 3: Religious and Political Considerations**
- Saudi Arabia's role as custodian of Islam's holiest cities creates particular concern about religious credibility
- Conservative approach on cryptocurrency appeals to conservative religious constituencies
- CBDC development positions Saudi Arabia as Islamic finance technology leader
- Combination (restrict private crypto, develop sovereign CBDC) sends message: Saudi Arabia leads fintech innovation through Islamic principles and sovereign control

### 6.3 The Unregulated Market Reality

#### 6.3.1 Market Data and the Regulatory Gap Problem

**Cryptocurrency Adoption Despite Prohibition:**
- Estimated Saudi users: 300K-500K (out of 36M population)
- Estimated monthly trading volume: $200-400 million USD
- Primary exchange platforms: Binance, Kraken, Bybit (all international, unregulated in Saudi Arabia)
- Primary use case: Speculative trading (not payments or productive use)
- Regulatory status: All trading is technically off-books and outside SAMA oversight

#### 6.3.2 The Regulatory Paradox: Why Prohibition Without Regulation Fails

Saudi Arabia's approach demonstrates a critical regulatory principle: **Prohibition without regulatory alternative creates uncontrolled unregulated market**, with worse outcomes than conditional regulation.

**Mechanism:**
1. SAMA prohibits (implicitly) by not licensing any exchanges
2. Users who want crypto access migrate to offshore platforms
3. SAMA loses all oversight capability
4. No consumer protection, no anti-fraud oversight
5. No Shariah board monitoring
6. Users face maximum gharar and maysir exposure (unregulated markets have no position limits, leverage restrictions, or Shariah screening)

**Comparative Analysis:**
- Malaysia (regulated with conditions): Users have position limits, leverage controls, Shariah screening
- Bahrain (regulated stablecoins): Users have 100% reserve backing ensuring value stability
- Saudi Arabia (prohibited, unregulated): Users have zero protections on international exchanges

**Counterintuitive outcome:** Saudi Arabia's prohibition approach actually creates MORE maysir-like risk (pure speculation on unregulated platforms) compared to conditional regulatory approaches in Malaysia/Bahrain.

#### 6.3.3 Brain Drain and Competitive Disadvantage

Another consequence of prohibition without alternative: crypto professionals and fintech companies relocate to jurisdictions with clearer regulatory frameworks.

**Observable Trend (2023-2026):**
- Saudi fintech startups moving to Dubai (UAE is clear VARA framework)
- Saudi crypto entrepreneurs establishing Singapore operations (clearer regulatory path)
- Saudi software engineers and product managers hired by fintech companies in Bangkok, Kuala Lumpur, and Dubai
- Saudi Arabia losing talent in emerging digital asset sector while competitors gain expertise and economic activity

**Competitive Impact:** While Saudi Arabia restricts private cryptocurrency, neighboring UAE becomes regional fintech hub. Regulatory arbitrage drives talent and capital flows away from Saudi Arabia toward more permissive jurisdictions.

### 7.2 Shariah Screening Mechanisms Compared

**Malaysia's Approach: Multi-Criteria Assessment**

**UAE/VARA's Approach: Tiered Risk Assessment**
- Five maqasid dimensions mapped to operational criteria

Each framework operationalizes anti-maysir principles through different mechanisms:

**Malaysia: Regulatory Safeguards**
- Position limits (5%)

- 24-hour cooling periods after losses
- Prohibited products (perpetual futures, options, structured products, crypto lending)
- Strength: Comprehensive protection architecture
- Weakness: Relies on user understanding of risks; education still planned

**Bahrain: Structural Prevention**
- Reserve backing eliminates speculation incentive (fixed 1:1 price)
- Derivatives prohibited
- Reserve audits provide transparency
- Monthly public disclosure
- Strength: Speculation is structurally impossible (fixed price)
- Weakness: Limited to stablecoins; conservative scope

**UAE/VARA: Layered Protection with Education**
- Position limits (5% Tier 1, 2% Tier 2)
- No retail leverage (1:1)
- Mandatory education (30-minute exam before trading Tier 2)
- Daily stop-loss (-15% for Tier 1, -10% for Tier 2)
- Extended cooling periods (1 week after 25% loss)
- Tier 3 restrictions for highest-risk assets
- Strength: Combines structural limits with user education
- Weakness: Education requirement adds friction to entry

**Indonesia: Pending Full Framework**
- Position limits (5%)
- No retail leverage (1:1)
- Mandatory Shariah board approval (framework still developing)
- Insurance requirement for custodians
- Strength: Regulatory intent is protective
- Weakness: Shariah framework delays create interim uncertainty

**Saudi Arabia: No Protections**
- No investor protection framework (no regulatory pathway)
- Users rely entirely on offshore platform protections
- Weakness: Users face maximum risk with zero institutional oversight

## 9. CHAPTER CONCLUSION: FROM THEORY TO IMPLEMENTATION

This chapter has documented how five Muslim-majority jurisdictions have translated Chapter 1's abstract jurisprudential framework into concrete regulatory policy. Key findings:

**Finding 1: Regulatory Diversity Reflects Institutional Context**

No uniform regulatory model exists. Five distinct approaches emerged reflecting:
- Institutional maturity (Malaysia most developed, Indonesia transitional)
- Market stage (Bahrain nascent stablecoin market vs. Malaysia mature crypto market)
- Political economy (Saudi Arabia prioritizes SAMA control, Malaysia permits independent Shariah board)

**Finding 2: Successful Frameworks Share Three Structural Features**

Malaysia, Bahrain, and UAE (most operationally mature) all include:
